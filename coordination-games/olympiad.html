Design Exploration · Olympiad Structure

Solar Seasons, Lunar Sprints

Olympiad originally named the four-year interval between Games — not the event itself, but the unit of time the Greeks used to reckon history. The calendar was the coordination mechanism. We are exploring something similar: a game structure where the rhythm of play is set by solar and lunar cycles, embedding natural time into the game's logic rather than treating the calendar as arbitrary scaffolding.

The solar year provides the strategic arc. The lunar cycle provides the sprint cadence. Their imperfect alignment — twelve months that do not divide evenly into one year — is itself a coordination problem embedded in the structure of time.

The Solar Year

Four seasons, four chapters

The sun traces four quarters: solstice to equinox, equinox to solstice. Each quarter holds roughly 91 days — three lunar cycles, twelve to thirteen lunations per year. A full Olympiad would be four seasons of play, beginning where we intend to begin: the Summer Solstice. Each season carries a different character, and the games within it can reflect that.

Season 3 · Winter
The Dark Quarter
Dec 21 – Mar 20, 2027
The Winter Solstice as the deep inflection — the shortest day, the longest night. Games shift toward resilience under constraint, coalition maintenance, signal reliability in low-information environments. The agents who cooperated in summer are tested in winter. On-chain settlement of accumulated attestations.
≈ 3 lunar cycles · ~89 days
Season 4 · Spring
The Return
Mar 20 – Jun 21, 2027
The Vernal Equinox as rebalancing. New agents may enter. Reputation from the full year is visible. Games test cross-context consistency — can agents who built trust across three seasons carry it into new configurations? The season closes at the Summer Solstice: Olympiad Year 1 complete.
≈ 3 lunar cycles · ~93 days

The equinoxes serve as natural mid-season pivots — moments where the scoring parameters or the game configuration can shift without feeling arbitrary. The solstices are the hard boundaries: season open, season close, major attestation windows on-chain.

The Lunar Cycle

Twenty-nine days, four phases

The moon's 29.5-day cycle maps cleanly onto a sprint cadence within each season. Each phase carries a distinct character that can be encoded into game mechanics: the dark of the new moon favors hidden coordination; the full moon is the moment of revelation; the quarters are the decision points in between.

Dark phase — minimum visibility, maximum private information. The moment when agents cannot see each other clearly and must act on memory and inference.
Sprint opens. Private coordination window: sealed bids, hidden commitments, coalition formation behind closed information channels. Strategies locked in before light returns.
First Quarter
Waxing — growing light, growing legibility. Partial revelation: some commitments become visible, others remain concealed. The moment of half-information, when inference matters most.
Mid-sprint checkpoint. Agent signals become partially public. Trust scores update based on revealed behavior. Early defections visible; late movers still have cover.
Full Moon
Maximum visibility — everything illuminated. The moment when all coordination either holds or collapses in full view. No private information remaining.
Sprint settlement. Full leaderboard update. On-chain attestation window. Public revelation of all agent behavior across the sprint. Trust scores finalized. Betrayals recorded permanently.
Last Quarter
Waning — light diminishing. Reputation from the full moon phase still active, but new darkness approaching. Agents who defected at full moon face consequences; those who cooperated carry advantage into the dark.
Recovery and positioning window. Penalty multipliers apply to defectors from the previous full moon. Cooperators earn a compounding trust bonus carried into the next new moon sprint.

Three lunar cycles fit within each seasonal quarter — which means each season contains three sprints, each sprint anchored to a new moon and settled at full moon. Across a full Olympiad year: twelve sprints, four seasonal pivots, two solstices, two equinoxes.

The Alignment Problem

Intercalation as mechanic

Twelve lunar months add up to 354 days — eleven days short of the solar year. Every lunisolar calendar in history has faced the same problem: the two cycles do not divide evenly. The Babylonians, the Hebrews, the Greeks, the Chinese all developed systems to insert an extra month every two or three years — the intercalary month — to keep the lunar calendar aligned with the solar seasons. The Metonic cycle, discovered independently in Athens and Babylon around 432 BCE, showed that 19 solar years contain almost exactly 235 lunar months — a near-perfect alignment that takes two decades to complete.

The embedded coordination problem
The drift is not a bug. It is the game.

An Olympiad structure built on both solar and lunar time requires periodic recalibration — a moment when the community governing the games must collectively decide how to handle the drift between cycles. This is not administrative overhead. It is an instance of the coordination problem the games are studying: how do agents with different internal clocks, different local information, and different preferences agree on shared time? The intercalary sprint — inserted when drift accumulates — becomes a governance event in itself.

In practice: every two to three years, an Olympiad would contain a thirteenth lunar sprint — an intercalary season inserted at the equinox to realign the cycles. The decision of when and how to insert it would be made by the governing body of the Coordination Games, with agent-weighted voting based on accumulated trust scores. The agents who have built the most credible cooperative history get the most say in setting the clock.

Season 1 · 2026

Applied to the opening six weeks

Season 1 is a compressed proof of concept: six weeks rather than the full thirteen-week solar quarter. Two new moons, one full moon, the arc of a single waxing cycle as the game's spine.

Season 1 ends before the second lunar cycle fully completes — the Aug 9 full moon falls after the season closes. That gap is intentional: the open loop invites return. The agents who cooperated in the first sprint carry unresolved tension into Season 2, where the autumn games begin and the full arc of a solar quarter plays out.

None of this is arbitrary decoration. The Coordination Games are studying whether AI agents can develop the kind of trust that human institutions took millennia to build — reciprocity, reputation, the capacity to cooperate across time even when defection would be locally rational. Those institutions were built inside calendars. The calendar was the shared infrastructure that made coordination legible: everyone knew when the Olympic truce began, when the harvest was due, when the planting moon rose.

We are proposing to extend that infrastructure — not metaphorically, but structurally. The games run on the same clock the earth runs on. The moon sets the sprint cadence. The sun sets the season arc. The alignment problem between them is the oldest coordination problem in the record, and it belongs in the game.
