Parnassus House at RegenHub · Session 1 Reading Plato · Republic Book II · 369B – 373E

The City in Speech

The systemic variables Socrates introduces as the city grows

This is a structural reading of the dialogue between Socrates and Adeimantus where the city is built up in logos, layer by layer. Each layer adds a new variable to the system. What looks at first like a story about specialization becomes, very quickly, a model of how needs cascade into roles, roles cascade into institutions, and institutions cascade into geopolitics.

Stephanus numbers (369b, 370a, etc.) are used as anchors so the parameterization is portable across translations. References here track the seminar reading and extend through the moment Glaucon rejects the healthy city as a "city of pigs," which is where the next session is likely to begin.

Phase I · The First City
Variables 1 through 4
Socrates founds the city on need and the principle of one person, one art.
V1369B

Self-sufficiency gap

The originating condition. No individual is self-sufficient; each falls short in many respects. This is the only premise Socrates needs to set the city in motion. Adeimantus accepts that there is no other principle for the foundation of a city.

Triggersthe entire system. Cities exist because individuals cannot meet their own needs alone.
V2369C

Primary needs hierarchy

Three needs are named in priority order: food first, then housing, then clothing and similar coverings. The order is consequential. Food is for being and living; the others follow.

Generatesthe minimum specialist roles in V3.
V3369D

Minimum viable specialist set

Four to five men: a farmer, a house-builder, a weaver, a shoemaker, and someone who looks after bodily needs. This is the absolute floor for a city to count as a city.

Open questionraised at this point: should each provide for himself across all four functions, or pool labor and let one farmer feed all four?
V4370A–C

One man, one art (the specialization principle)

Socrates settles the open question by combining three sub-claims that together form the operating principle of the city: humans differ in natural aptitude (370a), specialization yields more and finer goods (370c), and tasks have a kairos, a right moment that will not wait for the worker who is also doing other things (370b). Specialization is therefore not just efficient but kairologically necessary.

Consequenceevery later variable in this list. Once one-man-one-art is accepted, the city must add a new specialist for every new function it discovers.
Phase II · The Healthy City
Variables 5 through 12
The specialization principle compounds. Tools, animals, geography, exchange, and labor mismatches each force a new role.
V5370C–D

Tool dependency

The farmer cannot make his own plow well, nor the builder his own tools, nor the weaver and shoemaker theirs. Each primary art is parasitic on a tool-making art. Carpenters, blacksmiths, and other craftsmen enter the city as a necessary second tier.

Addscarpenters, blacksmiths, additional craftsmen.
V6370D–E

Animal labor

Oxen are needed for plowing, beasts of burden for carrying loads, sheep for wool, herds for leather. The city now also needs people to handle the animals.

Addsoxherds, shepherds, other herdsmen.
V7370E–371A

Geographic insufficiency

It is impossible, Socrates says, to found the city in a place where no goods need to be imported. Some inputs will always come from elsewhere. The city's perimeter is therefore not closed. This is a structural claim, not a contingent one. No site is autarkic.

Forcesthe import-export apparatus that follows.
V8371A

Trade reciprocity

An emissary going empty-handed comes back empty-handed. Imports are paid for with exports, which means the city must produce surpluses of the kind other cities want, not just what it itself wants. Internal production is now sized by external demand as well as internal need.

Addsmore farmers and artisans, beyond what local consumption alone would require.
V9371A

External-trade agents

People dedicated to the import-export function. Socrates calls them traders (emporoi). They operate between cities.

Addstraders.
V10371B

Maritime infrastructure (conditional)

If trade is by sea, a further class of seafarers is required. This is one of the few branching variables in the model. The city's geographic position determines whether maritime expertise is part of its stack.

Addssailors and seafarers, where applicable.
V11371B

Marketplace and currency

Internal exchange among the city's own producers needs an institutional venue and a medium. Socrates introduces both at once, almost as a unit: a marketplace arises, and with it a system of currency to facilitate exchange. Money here is functional, not yet hoarded. It exists to fix the time-and-quantity asymmetry between producers.

Addsthe marketplace as institution and currency as medium.
V12371C–D

Retailers (the time-mismatch role)

A producer arriving at market may not find a counterparty wanting to exchange at that moment. Sitting idle would violate V4 (the kairos rule and the specialization principle). The system therefore needs people who specialize in waiting in the market and exchanging money for goods and goods for money. Socrates marks them as the least able-bodied class, those unsuited to other work. Retailers (kapeloi) are distinguished from traders (V9): retailers stay in the market, traders move between cities.

Addsretailers, and a class distinction between internal and external commerce.
V13371E

Wage labor

A residual class: those whose intellectual contribution Socrates judges marginal but whose physical strength is usable. They sell the use of that strength for a wage. This is the first appearance of labor as commodity in the city, distinct from labor as practice of an art.

Addswage-earners (misthotoi).
Phase III · The Healthy City Closes
Variable 14
Socrates declares the city complete and locates justice in the relations among the parts.
V14371E–372C

The healthy city as steady state

The city is now grown to full size. Adeimantus is asked where justice is, and answers, perhaps, in the need the groups have for one another. Socrates describes the lifestyle: bread, wine, simple clothing and shoes, modest feasts of cakes and onions and figs, garlands and songs, families sized to resources, lives lived in peace and health, ending in old age and bequeathed to descendants. This is the true city, in Socrates' phrase, the healthy one.

Steady statereciprocity of needs as the binding force; war, growth, and luxury all absent.
Phase IV · The Feverish Turn
Variables 15 through 19
Glaucon refuses the healthy city. Each new appetite forces a new tier of variables.
V15372C–E

Demand for relish (the appetite shift)

Glaucon calls the healthy city a city for pigs and demands the conventional comforts: couches, tables, relishes, desserts. Socrates accepts the move because, he says, examining a luxurious city may help locate justice and injustice as they actually arise. The model now has to absorb appetite that exceeds need.

Triggersthe entire feverish-city expansion that follows.
V16373A–B

Aesthetic and luxury production

Painting and embroidery, gold and ivory, perfumes and incense, courtesans and cakes, all in endless variety. Necessities (housing, clothing, shoes) are no longer designated as such. The system multiplies categories of artisans for objects that exist for their own sake.

Addsa long list: hunters, imitators of all kinds — poets, rhapsodes, actors, choral dancers, contractors, makers of ornaments. Socrates lingers on this list deliberately.
V17373C

Personal-service infrastructure

Once luxury is in the system, the bodies of citizens are themselves objects of cultivation. Wet nurses and nurses for children, beauticians and barbers for adults, cooks and relish-makers for the table, swineherds for the meat the older city did not need.

Addsservice workers attending to bodies and households.
V18373C–D

Health-system load

The luxurious lifestyle generates illness the simpler lifestyle did not. The city now needs far more doctors than before. Medicine, in this reading, is partly a downstream cost of luxury, not a baseline good.

Addsa substantially larger medical class.
V19373D–E

Territorial pressure and war

The expanded population and appetite outgrow the original land. The city must take a slice of the neighbor's territory; the neighbor will do the same in return if they have followed the same path. War follows. Socrates marks this carefully: he does not say yet whether war is good or bad, only that the model has now discovered the origin of war. War is endogenous to the feverish city, not imported from outside.

Forcesa further expansion: the city must now grow by an entire army, which sets up the introduction of the guardian class in the next reading.

What the parameterization reveals

Three patterns are worth flagging for whoever returns to the text next.

The chain is strict. Each variable is forced by the one before. Once V4 (one man, one art) is accepted, every subsequent need that the city discovers has to become someone's full-time role. The city does not have the option to assign a need to someone's spare time. This is what makes the cascade compound so quickly. By V12 the city already contains primary producers, secondary craftsmen, animal handlers, external traders, maritime workers, retailers, and wage-earners — all derived from four founding roles plus a single principle.

Two variables introduce institutions, not roles. V11 (marketplace and currency) is the only place in the healthy city where Socrates adds shared infrastructure rather than another worker. Money appears here as a coordination device for the time-and-quantity asymmetry of exchange, not yet as a store of value or instrument of accumulation. The instinct to move from a labor-mismatch to an institutional-fix is the same instinct REA accounting formalizes much later: needs that cannot be met by adding agents are met by adding events and a unit of measure.

The break happens at V15. Everything from V1 to V14 is inside a logic of need. V15 changes the operative variable from need to appetite, and the system response from differentiation to multiplication. The healthy city differentiates roles to meet needs more finely. The feverish city multiplies roles to satisfy desires that have no natural ceiling. The discovery in the room — that pleonexia is the variable Plato cannot find an internal regulator for — lands precisely at this transition. The model parameterizes it cleanly: V15 is the input, V19 (war) is the output, with V16, V17, and V18 as the load along the path.
