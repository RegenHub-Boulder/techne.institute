Techne and Civilization — Session I

Opening the series

The series will move chronologically through how thinkers have made sense of technology. AI is forcing a reckoning with problems the tradition has known about for a long time, that technology is ambivalent, hard to govern, and full of downstream consequences. The intent is to surface the assumptions that structure the typical arguments and to ask how a serious response might look.

The Greek word techne descends from an Indo-European root meaning something like dwelling or constructed abode. It is the root of technology, technique, technician. Beginning with the Greeks is beginning with the first sustained inquiry into what technique is and what it does to those who use it.

The myth of Thoth and Thamos

The first reading came from the Phaedrus. Socrates tells a myth about an Egyptian god, Thoth, who arrives at the court of King Thamos with a portfolio of newly devised arts: number, calculation, geometry, astronomy, dice, and finally written letters. Thoth presents writing as a pharmakon, a drug for memory and wisdom. Thamos refuses to ratify the claim.

You have found a drug not for memory but for reminding. You are supplying the opinion of wisdom to the students, not truth. For having become hearers of much without teaching, they will seem to be sensible judges in much, while being for the most part senseless and hard to be with, since they have become wise in their own opinion instead of wise.

The myth contains two distinct moves. The first is the distinction between memory and reminding, the inner faculty and the external prompt. The second is the displacement of wisdom by the appearance of wisdom. Possessing books, or the papyri of the time, is mistaken for possessing what books contain.

The room recognized the critique immediately. It is the structure of a familiar contemporary worry. When the cognitive load is offloaded to a tool, the muscles atrophy, but the user feels more capable because the output looks fluent and coherent.

What counts as natural

The discussion turned to whether speech and writing belong to the same kind of activity. They do not. Spoken language is acquired osmotically inside a developmental window; if a child does not learn to speak in that window, real cognitive damage follows. Reading and writing require deliberate effort, sustained pressure on children, motor training that wires the brain in a particular way. Numeracy follows literacy and depends on it. None of this is natural in the sense of being spontaneous or organic.

That word "natural" was contested, and rightly. Lifting weights takes effort and produces a body that is not exactly natural, yet harnesses natural powers. Pascal warned that the modern European, drilled in mathematics from childhood, looks like a person who has trained one quad at the gym, hyper-developed and unbalanced. The point is not that effort is bad but that effort is not the same thing as fit. The arts we depend on do not come pre-installed.

From here a sharper claim followed. If literacy already strains a faculty we do not possess by nature, then every later technique piled on top of literacy adds further strain. Other animals carry the intelligence to live with the techniques they depend on; beavers do not go to school to build dams. Humans, by contrast, parachuted alone into the Colorado wilderness, could not reconstitute fire, agriculture, or shelter from memory. The arts that most condition our survival are not in us. That is a peculiar position for an animal to be in.

The multivalence of techne

One participant pushed back on the implicit pessimism. The world keeps getting better in many ways. Some appetite for expansion seems intrinsic to being alive. Why call this destructive?

Plato's answer is not that techne is bad. It is that techne is multivalent. Every innovation unlocks an upside, and the upside is real, especially for those who learn the art well. But it also unlocks costs, some internal, some external, often very hard to track, with downstream consequences that propagate through the community in ways that are difficult to predict and frequently destabilizing. Plato will not call this progress, if by progress one means a unidirectional ratchet. Politics on this view is cyclical, not linear.

Civilizations that have appeared in the historical record have, without exception, declined. The platonic explanation is that we lack the genetic intelligence to moderate the abundance our techniques generate. Once a population produces a little surplus, it resets its expectations. It comes to expect a more pleasant world than nature supplies. The Greeks called this disposition pleonexia, the desire to have more than one needs. Pleonexia drives expansion, attracts marauders, requires defensive technique, generates oligarchy, undoes the restrictive law in favor of permissive law, opens new menus of pleasure, and washes away the structures the city depended on. The cycle does not seem to be escapable from inside the cycle.

One reframing came from the floor. The pattern looks like a security dilemma. A population content with what it has cannot afford that contentment if its neighbors are not content. Bronze outcompetes stone. The agriculturalists are obligate competitors with the cyclopeans at the gate. So the path dependence is not chosen exactly; it is forced by the field.

The city in speech

The second reading came from Republic Book II. Socrates founds the city in speech from the simplest premise.

A city, as I believe, comes into being because each of us is not self-sufficient but is in need of much. So when one man takes on another for one need and another for another need, and since many things are needed, many men gather in one settlement as partners and helpers, and to this common settlement we give the name city.

Two needs are present at the founding, not one. We need each other socially, the nervous system regulates in the company of others, and we need each other epistemically, because no one knows enough alone. The two needs are not perfectly harmonious. The social need would be satisfied in a tribe of kin. Cities exist because tribes cannot do certain things, especially extractive technique. Law is what cities use to address the tensions that emerge once kin bonds can no longer carry the weight.

Socrates then proposes specialization: one man, one art. The argument for it is that yields are higher, work is finer, and the crucial moment is not missed. Adeimantus is briefly tempted by the alternative, autarky, the self-sufficient household. Socrates closes that door. Specialization wins.

Specialization opens a question the dialogue treats lightly but the room did not. Are humans actually distributed by nature into the roles a complex division of labor requires? If next year the country runs short on plumbers, will nature deliver the right number of natural plumbers? The market, of course, is the modern device for solving this, prices rise, labor reallocates. But the question underneath the market is whether the roles themselves correspond to anything in human nature, or whether they are simply niches that exogenous motivation, pay, status, necessity, conscripts people into. To the degree that the roles require external motivation, they are not natural in the Platonic sense, and the fit between person and function is not given but constructed.

Money, abundance, and the strain on the social

Money entered the discussion as one of the great human inventions, an artificial equalizer that makes asymmetric goods commensurable. Apples and roofing shingles do not naturally proportion. A common medium solves the problem. But money does not spoil. It can be hoarded. It permits accumulation past the natural ceiling that perishable goods imposed, and the accumulation reshapes the social field in ways the social field is not naturally equipped to handle.

The same dynamic applied at the level of innovation itself. Innovators are a small subset of any population. Their rewards scale, while the talent does not fall under a Gaussian distribution. The asymmetry compounds. The kin-network can absorb a great deal of friction because emotional bonds carry the tension. As the group grows past that scale, the bonds cannot. Justice, in the formal sense, becomes necessary precisely because cities cannot be friends. Citizenship is the best one can do.

The cybernetic condition

The conversation widened to the present. One participant offered the now-familiar framing that we live with godlike technology, medieval institutions, and paleolithic wetware. The further techne evolves, the more these layers telescope apart. The gap is what capital exploits. It is also the gap that wise use of technique is trying to close from the other direction, by looking back and integrating across the layers rather than racing ahead of them.

A second framing landed in the same place. Capitalism takes the thing humans naturally want and strips it down to its barest essence, then pumps it up to eleven. Sex becomes pornography. Friendship becomes followers. Food becomes high-fructose corn syrup. None of these substitutes is what was wanted, but each is more stimulating than the original along the narrow axis it has been engineered to optimize. Most people do not know they are inside the trade-off because the unstripped version is no longer on the menu.

The room resisted a clean binary on AI. The frame of "leaning in" or "leaning out" misses the more interesting distinction, which runs between people using the tools in a way that augments their faculties and people, sometimes the same people, losing balance inside them. Some of the most grounded perspectives in the room belonged to people who use AI carefully. Some belonged to people who do not use it at all. The variable is the relationship to the technique, not the presence of the technique.

Living with techne

What, then, is the platonic counsel? Not anarcho-primitivism. Not Tyler Durden walking through the ruins of the financial district in a rawhide suit. The good life is in cities, with access to the goods of an economy. The task is to live in that economy without being mentally captive to it.

This is the function of the cave. Most humans, on the platonic picture, end up NPC-like inside the cave because the economy attaches their desires to its products and opinions. The cave is not a place; it is a configuration of attention. The way out is not literal exit. It is the cultivation of a self-awareness rigorous enough to extract oneself from the most pernicious illusions while remaining inside the city. Plato calls this philosophy. In an oral culture with five thousand years of stable cultivation, that work might be carried by the customs themselves. In a culture innovating faster than its institutions can metabolize, the work falls to the individual and to the small circles of friends within which it can actually be done.

The session closed roughly there, with a thread left open for the next: if cities cannot be friends, and the work of integration falls to small groups, what is the form of practice that lets a small group do that work without becoming either a sect or a node in the same extractive logic it is trying to step outside of.
