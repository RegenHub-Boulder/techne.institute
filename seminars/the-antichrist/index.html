The Antichrist

When relief becomes the problem

This is a thematic edit of the seminar transcript. The conversation moved quickly across many voices, often overlapping; rather than attribute lines to participants, the edit preserves the substance of the inquiry as it unfolded. References to Dostoevsky and to Thiel are paraphrased rather than reproduced. Paul Diduch and Niko Kovacevic co-facilitated. This was the closing session of the public series.

From Hagans to the Antichrist

The fourth and final session of the public series opened with brief returns to last week's reading. Nate Hagans had just released a podcast piece introducing the idea of a default mode network in society, picked up in part from Erik Davis's writing on cultural shifts in the 1970s. The question that lingered, more substantively, was whether the superorganism Hagans had described could be reconciled with the runaway intelligence Land had described in the prior session. Hagans thinks in terms of metabolism and energy. Land thinks in terms of intelligence. The two might be correlates, but Hagans was not asking the AGI-shaped question. He was asking the carbon-pulse-shaped question, with oil pumping through the vasculature of the body he was diagnosing.

The session then pivoted to the closing reading of the series: Ivan Karamazov's Rebellion and The Grand Inquisitor chapters from Dostoevsky's Brothers Karamazov, paired with Peter Thiel's appropriation of the Inquisitor image as a warning about world governance. The two texts together do something the previous three pairings had not done. They turn the question of technology into a question about evil, about the institutions that promise to relieve human suffering, and about whether the promise of relief is itself the danger.

The problem of evil, and Ivan's rebellion

The technical entry point was the problem of evil, the philosophic question of how to reconcile the existence of a good God with the existence of gratuitous suffering. Ivan Karamazov's version of the rebellion is not really atheism. He says he wants to believe and in some sense does believe. What he cannot accept is the cosmic ledger. The world contains too much suffering, particularly the suffering of innocent children, for any divine scheme of redemption to balance. Ivan wants to return the ticket. He does not refuse God's existence; he refuses to participate in the world on the terms it has been offered.

The discussion pressed the structure of the argument. Ivan needs an assumption about how things ought to be in order to register his complaint that they are not. That ought has to come from somewhere. The room walked through the Humean point that one cannot derive an ought from an is. If morality is grounded in nature and natural selection, the suffering of children is a byproduct of the same process that produced our capacity to call it suffering, and the moral charge collapses. Pro-social behavior can emerge convergently through evolution, but its emergence does not give it normative force. Ivan would say to this that naturalism is inadequate as a foundation for morality. You can identify many examples of cooperation in nature without producing a single example of an obligation.

The dilemma Ivan wants you to feel is the conjunction. The suffering is morally awful. The basis for calling it morally awful is not available. The very fact of the suffering threatens any moral cogency you might want to deploy against it. He wants you torn on both horns.

The Grand Inquisitor: relieving the burden Christ failed to manage

The Grand Inquisitor chapter follows from this. Ivan tells his brother Alyosha a poem he has composed in his head. Christ returns to sixteenth-century Spain during the Inquisition. The Grand Inquisitor recognizes him, has him arrested, and visits him in his cell to deliver a long monologue. The substance of the monologue is a charge against Christ himself.

The charge runs through the three temptations. After his baptism, Christ was led into the desert to face Satan. Satan offered three things: bread, miracle, and authority over the kingdoms of the world. Christ refused all three. The Inquisitor's claim is that he should have accepted. Humans, in the Inquisitor's reading, are not capable of bearing the freedom Christ left them with. We are dismal animals, half slave and half rebel, confused and brutal, and to ask us to be Christlike is to ask us to do something our nature cannot sustain. Christ left us with a freedom too heavy to carry. What we needed was to be relieved of that freedom, fed and entertained, awed by miracle, ordered by authority, given a religious structure that takes the moral weight off us and places it somewhere else. The Roman Catholic Church, in the Inquisitor's telling, has corrected Christ's mistake. The Church gives humans what they actually need. The Church is what Christ should have built.

The room paused on this. The Inquisitor's framing is cynical at the surface but motivated, in his own self-understanding, by a kind of care that he regards as more honest than the burden Christ imposed. The most interesting move in his speech is that the relief he is providing is itself the inversion he is grateful to have inherited. The Church inherited the bargain from the desert. It accepted the three things Christ refused. It built the structure on the inversion.

A connection surfaced to Daniel Schmachtenberger's framing of hell as a protector meme: a belief that exists to keep people inside a wider system of beliefs by threatening eternal suffering to anyone who questions it. The notion of original sin, paired with the threat of hell, creates a bind for the individual that resembles a gun held to the head. The Protestant Reformation rebelled against this structure on the grounds that the gospels do not warrant it. Ivan is not a Protestant. He is making a different move. He is not protesting that the Church misread the gospel. He is suggesting that the Church corrected the gospel, and that this correction was the right thing to do.

From the Inquisitor to Thiel: the Antichrist as world governance

The pivot to Peter Thiel runs through this same image, updated. Where Ivan's Grand Inquisitor stood for the Catholic Church of the sixteenth century, Thiel sees the same shape today in the prospect of a single world government, enabled and reinforced by modern technology. The Catholic Church promised bread, miracle, authority, and absolution at the scale of medieval Europe. A planetary state with modern technological capacity could promise the same things at the scale of the entire species, and far more powerfully. The relief on offer would be unprecedented. So would the loss.

Thiel's frame is that we are in a moment of unusually high existential risk and unusually high technological capacity at the same time. The risks pull us toward consolidation, because consolidation promises safety. The capacities make consolidation feasible. The combination is what Thiel calls the Antichrist, drawing the term from the same Christian eschatological tradition Dostoevsky was working in. The Antichrist's slogan, Thiel notes from Thessalonians, will be peace and safety. The slogan is the warning. It is exactly what people will want, and exactly what should signal that something has gone wrong.

The room held this with care. Thiel is not asking us to be skeptical of peace or safety as goods. He is asking us to be skeptical of the structure that would promise them at the scale that would make them universally available. If the price is the universal homogeneous state, the price is too high. The room used the placeholder Zuckistan for the worst version of this: a planetary Meta in which Mark Zuckerberg is god-emperor, everyone is fed and safe, no one is fighting, and the cost is the surrender of every other thing the freedom Christ left us with had been for.

The Girardian frame

Behind Thiel's reading sits René Girard's account of the scapegoat mechanism, which Thiel takes seriously. The Girardian thesis is that human societies have historically maintained coherence by periodically identifying an outgroup, condemning it, making it suffer, and then, after the fact, recognizing that the victim was innocent. The cycle repeats. A savior figure emerges at the end of each cycle to redeem the scapegoating; the savior reveals the innocence; a new cycle begins. Christ, in Girard's reading, broke the loop. He made the scapegoat mechanism visible as such. Once visible, it cannot be re-mystified. There cannot be another savior because the lesson has been taught.

The consequence Girard draws from this is grim. If the scapegoat mechanism is broken, the periodic discharge of violence that kept societies coherent is no longer available. The pressure does not vanish; it accumulates. Without the cyclical reset, the next discharge is apocalyptic in scale. Girard's apologetic, then, is Christian. The lesson has to be learned and lived, or the alternative is not the old cycle but something much worse.

Thiel is a Girardian, and the inheritance shows. He takes the apocalyptic potential seriously. He treats Christian apologetics as a live resource for thinking about politics. He also, the room noted, invests in companies that do not obviously square with the apologetic. The contradiction is where most of the rest of the session went.

Cake eaters and bullet biters

A piece of philosophic vocabulary came in to organize the contradiction. A bullet biter is someone who accepts the conclusion of an argument even when the conclusion is unwelcome. A cake eater is someone who wants the logically endorsed outcome and the emotionally satisfying one at the same time. The room read Thiel, charitably, as a cake eater.

The deeper structure of his cake-eating is that he believes growth is a precondition for the functioning of any polity. If GDP contracts, if technology recedes, the world becomes zero-sum or negative-sum, and that condition is far worse than the conditions of growth even if growth carries risks. Once you accept growth as non-negotiable, the marriage of Christian apologetics and capitalist strategy becomes very difficult to sustain cleanly. Thiel knows this. When asked about it directly he tends to deflect, often with a version of "I don't like hypotheticals." The room read the deflection as honest in its way. He has identified a fault line in Western civilization that genuinely does not resolve. If we keep growing, we may end up in Zuckistan. If we stop, we may end up somewhere even worse.

The room was sympathetic to this difficulty without being convinced that Thiel has the right response to it. The energy-blindness frame from Session 3 surfaced again as a parallel. We are growth-blind in the same way we are energy-blind. Our expectations about the future presume continued growth in a way we cannot easily inspect. Asking us to imagine a world without growth is asking us to imagine something our institutions, our retirement accounts, and our parental expectations are not designed for.

Third ways: d/acc, decentralization, the security tradeoff

The natural exit from the bind is a third way, neither apocalyptic collapse nor universal homogeneous state. Thiel himself has gestured toward this in the quote about AI being totalitarian and crypto being libertarian. The room walked through what a third way would actually require.

The framework picked up from the broader cooperative conversation is d/acc: decentralized, accelerationist, democratic, defensive. The fourth term, defensive, does most of the work. The idea is that civilization can live at the blast radius of any single centralized government if its defensive capacity is increased faster than its offensive capacity. The strategy keeps the door open to decentralization without requiring renunciation of advanced technology. It is the framework most often invoked in the cooperative's circles as the candidate third way.

The room flagged the weak link in d/acc. If AI really is on the way to godlike capability, where a single actor could in principle synthesize a nuclear or biological weapon at the touch of a button, the defensive part of the framework has to be very robust. Thiel would worry that defense will not be perfect on the first round, and that the first failure could be unrecoverable. The argument for Zuckistan, in this telling, is the argument from fear: people will cash in their chips at the first credible threat of Armageddon, because the alternative looks worse. The defensive third way has to be more credible than the safety the centralized state can offer, or the centralized state wins by default.

The Schmittian and Straussian heritage Thiel draws on also surfaced here. Thiel uses the term katechon, the Pauline figure who holds back the Antichrist in Second Thessalonians. The katechon is a structural position, not a moral one. Anything powerful enough to hold back the Antichrist is also powerful enough to become the Antichrist. Palantir is a katechon. Palantir could also be the Antichrist. The United States government is a katechon. The United States government could also be the Antichrist. The strategy, on this view, is to pit multiple katechons against each other so that none of them ever consolidates fully. This is one way to read Thiel's investment record: he is not betting on any one company to be right; he is betting on diversification to prevent any one company from becoming everything.

The esoteric layer and the question of sincerity

The conversation drifted, near the middle, into the more difficult question of how seriously to take Thiel's stated positions. Several participants noted that the public Thiel and the investing Thiel do not always behave like the same person. He critiques ubiquitous technological surveillance and invests in companies that build it. He warns against the Antichrist and funds the katechons that could become it. He talks about the importance of human liberty and makes substantial investments in defense contractors and database infrastructure with deep ties to the security state.

One reading, offered from the floor, is that this is double-dipping. Another is that it is the consistent position of someone who believes growth and security must trade off, and that the trade-off is being made on the basis of strategic realism rather than moralism. The Straussian distinction between esoteric and exoteric writing was raised, the idea being that some truths are too dangerous for general circulation and that the public-facing communications of a Straussian thinker may not always be sincere in the conventional sense.

A more pointed thread followed, raising the long arc of post-9/11 surveillance: Palantir's role in the consolidation of federal data, the more recent investment in Carbyne (the Israeli emergency response company connected to former defense minister Ehud Barak and previously to Jeffrey Epstein), and the broader question of whether the figures shaping the global surveillance infrastructure are reducible to a coherent intention. The participant who raised this acknowledged that it sits at an esoteric layer beyond their pay grade. The room held the questions open without resolving them. They probably cannot be resolved from the outside. What can be said is that the public position and the investment record are not obviously the same position, and that the gap between them is a fact worth registering when reading anything Thiel writes.

The room landed on a more sympathetic reading. Thiel is not a psychopath using Christian apologetics as cover for an empire. He is a tormented person who actually believes most of what he says, who knows that his investments contribute to the very phenomenon he warns against, and who has decided that the strategic realism of pitting forces against each other is more responsible than either pure withdrawal or pure submission to a centralized solution. This is a reading that holds Thiel's contradictions as contradictions rather than as concealment, and it is the reading most compatible with the seriousness of his relationship to Girard.

The kiss

The final scene of the Grand Inquisitor was held to the end of the discussion, as the room felt it should be. After the Inquisitor's long speech, Christ does not respond in words. He kisses the Inquisitor on the mouth and is let out into the streets of the city. The Inquisitor, in Ivan's telling, feels the power of the kiss but refuses to be moved by it. He remains loyal to the idea.

The room read the kiss carefully. The temptation is to take it as a gesture of forgiveness. It is something more specific than that. The kiss is the assertion that what loves the individual human is not the same thing as what loves humanity. The Inquisitor loves humanity in the abstract. He has organized his entire institution around that love. Christ's kiss is a refusal of that institutional love and a direct return to the person in front of him. He does not argue. He does not refute. He looks at the Inquisitor as a person, and the Inquisitor cannot bear it. The Inquisitor is not moved because what would move him is precisely what his institution exists to abolish.

The implication, the room agreed, is the most difficult turn in the whole reading. Concern for humanity is not the solution to the Grand Inquisitor's problem. Concern for humanity is the Grand Inquisitor's problem. The humanitarian wants to manage the herd. The humanitarian wants to organize the relief of suffering at scale. The kiss is the alternative: the actual encounter with the individual person, the one who needs to be taken to the hospital, the one who can be seen rather than counted. The two are not commensurable, and confusing them produces the Inquisition.

This lands hard against the contemporary intuition. We think humanitarians are the good ones. We think loving humanity is what we should be doing. The Brothers Karamazov is saying, very directly, that this love produces stakes and chambers. The love that does not produce stakes and chambers is the love of the person in front of you, and only that love, and the willingness to be inconvenienced by it. The example used in the room was the homeless person on the street. Donating a hundred dollars to a nonprofit that addresses homelessness is not the same action as buying that person lunch and looking them in the eye. Both can be done, but they are not interchangeable, and the one that scales is not the one that loves.

Closing the arc

The session closed with practical announcements about the Q&A planned two weeks later, and with a brief return to a thread that had run through the whole series. Each of the four sessions had ended on a similar shape of question. Plato saw that humans have no genetic intelligence for moderating the abundance their techniques generate. Kingsnorth and Land disagreed about whether the resulting structure is built or grown but agreed that it exceeds any single agency. Hagans named the patient as the economy and biosphere together and made the carbon pulse legible as the energy substrate underneath. Dostoevsky and Thiel together, finally, named the most consequential structure that promises to relieve all of this: the planetary institution that would feed, entertain, and absolve the species at the price of its freedom.

The four readings converge. The thing that holds together is the thing that destroys what it was supposed to protect. The thing that promises peace and safety is the thing to be most wary of. The third way, whatever it turns out to be, has to honor the kiss. It has to keep the individual encounter at the center, because nothing else can carry the weight that the institution would otherwise ask to carry on the individual's behalf.

The closing image of the seminar, both literally and figuratively, was pizza. A coordinated delivery, sponsored partly by Coinbase and partly by Pizza DAO, distributed by a decentralized organization to people in a coworking space who had spent the afternoon discussing the Antichrist. The miracle, the mystery, and the authority were all faintly present in the joke. The kiss, by then, had been read.

Coda · After the session

The Christian statesman paradox

After the formal session closed, a smaller exchange continued the thread on Thiel's Christian framing. Thiel ends his Straussian Moment essay with a call for a Christian statesman. The smaller conversation flagged this as nearly a contradiction in terms. Jesus is not a statesman. The Sermon on the Mount is not a policy framework. To run the polis as a Christian, in any thoroughgoing sense, runs into a paradox the gospel itself does not resolve. The render-unto-Caesar formula is not a synthesis; it is Jesus refusing to provide the synthesis, telling the questioner that the two domains coexist without supplying any account of how they relate.

The Genesis layer of the paradox runs deeper than the gospel itself. Cain, after killing Abel, builds a city. The city in scripture begins as the construction that follows a sacrifice the builder did not get what he wanted from. There is a through-line from that founding moment to Jesus's refusal of the political offer in the desert. The moral logic of the socio-technical structures humans build is, in this reading, structurally inadequate to the moral longings that drove their building. Aquinas tried to synthesize politics and Christianity at the high medieval level. The room's wry observation was that the Grand Inquisitor is also a synthesis, and a successful one in its own terms, and that the successful synthesis is precisely the failure mode the original text was trying to name. The deeper observation, offered tentatively, was that even Girard himself would be put in an uncomfortable position if forced to face the dilemma directly. The contradiction does not belong only to Thiel. It belongs to the position.

A more charitable reading of the Christian-statesman position then surfaced. The work of the Christian polity, on this reading, is not to embody the Kingdom of Heaven on Earth but to keep the city from becoming the Grand Inquisitor. There has to be a clearing above the city, a window the city does not close, a Kingdom of Heaven the city looks up to without trying to absorb. The Christian statesman, on this reading, is the one who is self-limited by both halves of a divided soul, not fully a statesman because the Christian half refuses to let politics close the window, and not fully a Christian because the statesman half accepts that civilization requires what civilization actually requires. The contradiction is not a bug in the position. The contradiction is the position. The self-limiting tension is what prevents the politics from going feral.

A strange loop in contemporary politics was named in passing. Voters crave Christian presidents. The role of Christian statesman is structurally contradictory. The contradiction itself, rather than being a disqualification, is what some voters may be responding to, because the structurally contradictory office is the one most likely to be self-limited. Whether any actual occupant of the office honors the contradiction is a different question. The position is doing the work whether the occupant is doing it or not.

Thiel's own honesty about this surfaced in a moment from his Peter Robinson interview. He acknowledges that Mother Teresa is the greater Christian saint, and in the same breath argues that civilization still needs a Constantine. The acknowledgment is doing more work than it looks like it is doing. It is the position of a thinker who knows that the political form of Christianity, the form that built churches after the donation of Constantine and ran papal states under Julius II, is not the same form of life that the Sermon on the Mount describes. Civilization runs on Constantine. Sainthood runs on Mother Teresa. The two are not interchangeable, and no synthesis closes the gap without producing the Inquisitor.

The same paradox surfaces, the room noted, in Buddhism. The earliest texts ask the practitioner to give away everything and wander like the horned rhinoceros. No civilization is built on that instruction. Zen functions in part as an austere counter-current within the Buddhist tradition, resisting the elaborations that turned other forms of Buddhism into civilizational structures with political reach. The Dalai Lama is a living contradiction in the same shape as the Christian statesman. Previous Dalai Lamas, in their historical operation, organized state-scale civilization with the kind of duration and stability that the Buddha himself would not have recognized as continuous with his own teaching. The Pope sits in the same place. Read Machiavelli on Julius II, who was an active war-making pope, and the papal states become legible as the genuinely political form of the religion. The traditions that began as refusals of the city have, in every case the room could identify, ended up running cities. Whether this is failure or the only possible form of survival is the question the close of Session 4 had already been pointing at, and the coda after the session held it open without resolving it.
