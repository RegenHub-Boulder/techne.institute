The Superorganism — Hagans, the Carbon Pulse, and the Question of the Living Whole

From the machine to the organism

The third session continued the structure of the series. The first session worked the Greek view through Plato. The second worked The Machine through Kingsnorth and Land. This one worked The Superorganism through Nate Hagans. The reduction in one sentence: where Kingsnorth treats the thing supervening over us as a machine, and Land treats it as a runaway intelligence we cannot govern, Hagans treats it as an organism.

The framing distinction matters and the room was asked to hold it carefully. Kingsnorth's machine is constructed; corporate interests built it, even if it has slipped from any single hand. Land's teleoplexy is auto-catalytic but not living in any organic sense. Hagans presses a third frame. The thing we are inside is not built. It grew. It has a metabolism. It exchanges energy with its environment. It can be described in biological vocabulary because, on his view, that is what it actually is.

This shift has implications. If something is built, in principle the builders could be persuaded, opposed, or removed. If something grows of its own accord, the response has to be different. Hagans does seem to permit some scope for agency, but in tension with his analogy. An organism is not a thing its parts can decide to change. The room was warned to watch for that tension as the reading unfolded.

The patient: economy and biosphere as one body

Hagans opens the essay with a clinical framing. Modern civilization looks impressive on the surface — markets grow, planes fly, AI has arrived — but the vital signs are flashing red and many people are working on cures without first diagnosing the underlying condition. The patient, in his framing, is not any one of these systems. The patient is the fully coupled system of the global human economy and the Earth's biosphere, treated as one body.

The room paused on this. Marrying the economy and the biosphere as a single patient is a sizable move. Either the two systems really do constitute a coherent enough whole to share symptoms, or the analogy is doing more work than the substrate can bear. The room held the question open and let Hagans unfold his case.

One participant drew the room into a deeper substrate, sketching dissipative systems on the back of a seminar paper. Living systems are dissipative: they take in low-entropy inputs and emit high-entropy waste, sustaining themselves only as long as the input continues. Hurricanes work this way. Cells work this way. Artificial intelligence, on this framing, works this way too, with capital and GPUs and energy in, heat and waste out. Civilization scaled up exhibits the same structure. The framing matters because it grounds the organism metaphor in something more rigorous than intuition. Hagans does describe the superorganism as a dissipative engine, and the broader living-systems literature shares the vocabulary.

Energy blindness and the carbon pulse

Most people believe money powers the world, but Hagans wants to insist it is really energy. Animals were the first investors, spending calories to gain more. The surplus built organisms, then ecosystems, then human cultures, then civilizations. Two centuries ago, the world tapped the stored solar energy in coal, oil, and gas, and discovered that a single barrel of oil paired with a machine could do years of human labor for pennies. He calls this the carbon pulse: a one-time release of energy stored over geologic time, burned through in two centuries, which had taken millions of years to form. A trust fund, not a paycheck, and we have been throwing a planet-wide party on the strength of it.

The phrase Hagans uses for our relation to this is energy blind. We see the financial and technological outputs of the carbon pulse but not the inputs that power them. We mistake the tiny cost of fossil energy for its enormous value. We do not register pollution as a cost. We treat infinite growth on a finite planet as the normal condition.

The room agreed that energy blindness is real and underappreciated. The cyclist-and-toaster anecdote came in here. A world-class track cyclist with quads each as big as another person's torso can just barely sustain the wattage to toast bread, and only with a bicycle helping him. The picture of half a trillion human workers' worth of labor delivered each year by fossil energy is not visible to anyone who has not done this kind of math. The same point applies, increasingly, to artificial intelligence, where the experience of having minds available on tap masks the energy and compute underneath.

Emergence and the integrity question

The middle of Hagans's argument runs through emergence. The example he reaches for is the starling murmuration. A single starling follows three simple rules — stay close to your neighbors but not too close, and move toward the center — and from these rules a fluid unpredictable shape emerges in the sky. The murmuration is something the individual starlings did not design and cannot perceive from inside their own movement. This is emergence in nature, and Hagans wants to use it to set up the move to the human-economic case.

The room let the analogy work but pressed on its seams. Is a flock of starlings really an organism? It is something. It has a coherent pattern. But the parts-and-whole relation in a flock is not the same kind of parts-and-whole relation as in a starling's body. A wing is not separable from its starling in the way a starling is separable from its flock. The whole that is an individual being holds together by some internal principle that the murmuration does not obviously share.

The concept of holons surfaced as a way to think about this. A holon is a thing that is also a part of a larger whole. A person is a holon in a seminar, which is a holon in a city, which is a holon in a region, which is a holon in a watershed. Going up and down the holonic stack reveals that the relations are not the same at every level. Inside a body, organs are enclosed; their relation is one of structural integration. Inside a flock, starlings are networked; their relation is one of behavioral coupling, and they can separate without anyone dying. The endosymbiotic theory of mitochondria was raised as a place where the two kinds of relation actually meet: ancestrally independent organisms got consumed by a larger cell and now operate as integrated parts.

The question for Hagans, then, is which kind of whole the superorganism is. If it has the integration of a body, removing pieces would cause real damage, and his framing of debt and looming collapse makes sense. If it has the coupling of a flock, the metaphor still works but with much weaker force; pieces can leave the pattern without the pattern dying. The session did not settle which kind of whole Hagans is actually claiming.

Mindless but optimizing: where teleology breaks down

Hagans's key paragraph calls the superorganism mindless, unplanning, energy hungry, and emergent, and also driven by aligned incentives toward extraction and consumption. The room flagged a tension in the framing. Hagans wants the thing to be enough of an organism that its symptoms warrant alarm, and not so much of an organism that we have to ask about its mind, its agency, or its moral status.

The simplest organism can probably not be understood as mindless. Aristotle would have said even plants have soul — not a mind, but a clear purpose. Anything that metabolizes has a purpose: keep metabolizing. The criterion of organism that the modern living-systems literature uses — autopoiesis, dissipative structure, cognitive process — includes orientation toward an environment. If Hagans is granting the superorganism enough biology to be diagnosed, he may have to grant it enough teleology to be reckoned with. Otherwise the framing reads as defensive, treating it as alive when alarm helps the argument and as mechanism when the alarm would otherwise require addressing mind, evil, feeling, or other categories the essay would rather not enter.

The discussion landed on incentives as the place where the contradiction does most of its work. Hagans says the superorganism is driven by biological and cultural incentives. Incentives are simultaneously the spring out of which the system runs and the connective tissue that holds its parts together. But organs do not respond to incentives. The heart does not pump because it has been incentivized. Either incentives are something between distinct beings — in which case the superorganism is more like a market than a body — or they are the internal teleology of a single being, in which case the mindless framing is hard to sustain.

A more useful reframing offered from the floor was that the misalignment lives in the design of the ledger itself. Currency is the tool we built to map flows of energy through the system, and the logic of the ledger creates the incentive landscape that nudges human behavior. Redesigning the ledger could redirect the emergent patterns. This is closer to a Landian framing than to Hagans's own, but it does not have to be fatalistic. If the ledger is a designed artifact, it can be redesigned. Land's own skepticism about whether the ledger is intelligible at all sits as a check on the move, but the question is at least open.

The Ice Age trauma hypothesis

A longer thread followed on why the superorganism behaves the way it does. The framing offered drew on Joanna Macy's retelling of the Shambala warrior prophecy, in which the destructive patterns of present society are not the work of demons or extraterrestrials but emergent from human proclivities. Humanity, in this version, was so traumatized by the suffering of evolutionary scarcity — particularly through the Ice Age — that it carries a subconscious drive to subdue nature in order to insulate itself from the fear of annihilation. We over-fit on an era of evolutionary history. We have removed most of the scarcity but kept the consuming, because the hardware was wired for conditions that have passed.

A counter-reading from the Plato thread of Session 1 surfaced here. Plato would say something close to the inverse. Primitive societies feel more at home in nature; the ambient sense of terror is downstream of our growing dependence on agricultural technology and its complexity. The chicken-and-egg form of the question is whether the trauma drove the agriculture or the agriculture drove the trauma. Both readings agree that something is misaligned. They disagree about what is upstream.

A third move came in from the same direction. The more we understand our dependence on nature, the more fear we may carry, because we recognize how thoroughly we have domesticated ourselves out of any plausible return. A monkey released from a zoo back to the trees would probably fare okay. A modern human, by contrast, has been shaped into something it would be hard to revert. Rewilding the human is not symmetric to rewilding the monkey.

The great simplification and the techno-optimist pushback

The most contested move in Hagans's essay is what he calls the great simplification. The argument is that the carbon pulse will not last, that the costs of extracting hydrocarbons are already approaching the energy yields, and that the downslope of the pulse will mean less energy, less complexity, and less of the more that the superorganism has been built to want. He treats this as a when, not a maybe.

The room pushed back hard on the determinism in this framing. A version of Malthusian thinking is doing a lot of quiet work, and the historical record of Malthusian predictions is poor. The Neo-Malthusian movement of the 1970s predicted mass starvation by the 1990s. Peak oil predicted that fossil extraction would decline precipitously around the same period. Fracking emerged and rewrote that math. Nuclear is in a renaissance globally even if domestically constrained by regulation rather than technology. China is building reactors at a pace that will simply zoom past any constraint Hagans is naming. The argument that we always come up with the next fracking is not without force.

The room's most serious defense of Hagans came from his background. He was not a hippie. He came out of energy trading and developed his planetary theory in response to what he was seeing in actual markets. The cost per barrel was creeping up in ways that long enough timescales make legible. Fracking is itself evidence that the cheap reserves are gone; we would not be fracking if we did not have to. Whether this is sufficient evidence for the broader prediction is the contested point. But the claim is being made on the basis of specific market data, not pure intuition.

A balanced reading emerged. There are real ecological constraints. There are also real adaptations that historically rewrite where the constraints sit. The mistake is treating constraints as absolute when they are moving. The other mistake is treating adaptation as guaranteed when it has often required contingent breakthrough. Hagans's leveraged-bet framing landed well in this exchange. Either the complexity pays a dividend and we get to the stars, or we go bust. The math on whether renewable energy could pick up the mantle of the carbon pulse on the downslope was estimated, by a participant familiar with the numbers, at about a ten percent chance. Grim odds, but not zero.

Response paths: bend, not break

Hagans closes the essay with four categories of response: policy (biophysical realism, planning for bending not breaking), cultural (new stories, more trust, more social capital), community (mutual aid, local food and supply chains, ecosystem repair), and personal (skills, mindset, connections, readiness). The framing is hopeful in a measured way. He calls the moment a rite of passage rather than a crisis, and notes that the things that bring genuine joy and meaning — connection, purpose, time in nature, being of service — are not tied to material consumption. Once basic needs are met, what fulfills us is ancient. The lever, in this picture, is cultural change rather than collapse-avoidance: shift the story, and the wiring follows.

The room took the closing seriously without quite settling into it. One participant offered the opposite read: if there is only one carbon pulse, the move might be to spend it fully, to see how much complexity can be locked in before the simplification arrives. This is the accelerationist response transposed from Land into Hagans. The counter to it, from the same general direction, was to fork accelerationism into something more decentralized and more regenerative, embedding wisdom into the allocation rather than letting the Molochian dynamic run unmediated. Several in the room were sympathetic to this fork. It allows accelerationism's diagnosis without accepting its fatalism.

Closing: decentralization, the watershed, and Mars

The closing exchanges drifted across the question of where decentralization actually lives. Robin Hanson was raised as a thinker who treats interplanetary colonization as the gold standard, on the argument that global monoculture produces cultural drift and that multiple planetary bets would restore the variation evolution needs. The room found this both interesting and dark. The implication of bet-hedging at planetary scale is that the planet not picked can be allowed to burn, and the elites who make it off are released from obligation to the rest.

The counter, articulated from the seminar's host, is that the decentralization worth pursuing is bioregional rather than interstellar. The watershed is the unit. The work is to fall back in love with the Earth before imagining travel from it. We still do not know what is in the forest or the deep sea. The imagination of escape can obscure the work of getting to know the planet we are already on.

A physicist's estimate joined the exchange, by way of one participant's father: terraforming Mars would take about seven generations, and the first six would be hell, or at least underground. The actual experience of leaving the planet is unlikely to resemble the cinematic version. The image of going underground in Phoenix to wait out summer afternoons was offered as a foretaste of what living on Mars would actually be like. The lizards who slither out from under rocks when the temperature drops are doing what colonists would have to do, only with less margin.

The session closed on the bioregional thread. If the superorganism is a debt being called in, the response that scales is not just policy but practice: knowing the watershed, knowing the food chain, knowing the neighbors. The room broke for happy hour with the closing not quite resolved, which by this point in the series was its own kind of pattern.
