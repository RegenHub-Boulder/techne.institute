The Machine — Kingsnorth, Land, and the Question of the Runaway System

Opening the second session

The series is structured around motifs in technology. The first session worked the ancient view through Plato. This one took up The Machine, capital T, capital M. Two thinkers were chosen out of many that could have been. Paul Kingsnorth, whose recent book Against the Machine takes a position broadly skeptical of where the technological economy is taking us. And Nick Land, whose essay Teleoplexy presses an opposite move and asks whether what we call the machine is really a runaway intelligence we have already lost any handle on. The session held the two in tension and let the room work the difference.

The instruction was to read each thinker for the picture of the machine being fleshed out, where they agree, where they disagree, and whether a coherent picture comes into focus. Take neither framing on autopilot.

Approaching the machine: metaphor, abstraction, or emergent whole

Before any reading happened, the room was asked whether the machine framing actually resonates. It did, broadly, and the reasons surfaced quickly.

One thread was monetary. Money issuance and debt were named as the fundamental drivers of an economic system that has to metabolize value out of an unstructured natural world to keep itself running. The result is a kind of distributed optimization process running across many minds, asking what will generate market returns, with the machine existing partly to manufacture and meet the desires that result. Deleuze's term deterritorialization came up in this connection. The image is of value being stripped from its native context and converted into consumer goods, then circulated.

Another thread treated the machine as an emergent whole rather than a mechanism in any literal sense. Living systems do not behave like thermostats; turning a dial does not produce a determinate result. But certain large social systems have machinic properties, principal-agent dynamics, characteristic patterns and grammars, even if they are emergent rather than designed. The framing that won out for the discussion was that the economy is an artifice of craft, with its own internal logic, that we have to live with as our current solution to the problem of providing for shared shelter and food. More than metaphor, less than literally machine. An abstraction with coherent principles that can be discerned. This frame held for entering Kingsnorth, who treats the machine as something real enough to push back against.

Kingsnorth's lawnmower

Kingsnorth opens the chapter with a domestic story. He has tried to maintain a couple of acres in rural Ireland with hand tools, but at some point a man has to admit defeat. His children are not interested or available for a summer-long mowing team. He goes to the hardware shop. He does not pretend to have wanted to.

The room was asked why open this way. The lawnmower is technology, of course. But the salience is that Kingsnorth is also telling a story about his family, his land, and what it would mean for them to be integrated with their environment in a way that does not require Walmart. There is an implicit longing in the framing. Mammals, you might think, should be able to make do without the hardware store somehow. The fact that we cannot is the first datum.

Kingsnorth's headline claim is sharp: the labor and time saving technologies of his lifetime, from microwaves to the internet, have given things and taken things, but never actually saved time or labor. The room split on this. Some pushed back immediately. The claim, taken straight, looks false. Others found a charitable reading. If you account for the full lifecycle of a tool, the extraction, the manufacturing, the labor compounded across the supply chain, the time savings start to wash out. Another framing, more philosophical, was that the externalization of human capacities into tools dulls the relationship between the human and the natural world; each abstraction layer removes the user another step from underlying reality, with finance named as a specific case where someone might be five integrals removed from soil before realizing it.

The room also flagged the contradiction sitting on the page. Kingsnorth himself is the one going to the hardware store. He is granting that the trade-off exists, that he gets something out of the lawnmower, that scything with his kids is not just a moral choice but a labor choice with its own opportunity cost. His own children, on his framing, may have other things to do.

Kingsnorth's name for the situation is the Devil's Bargain. We embrace the new, lose the old, and find ourselves more deeply entwined in a technological web from which extrication, even if wanted, is no longer available. The phrase concedes the trade-off and registers the lament at the same time. What Kingsnorth seems to want to say is that we don't always know what we are giving up at the moment we accept the bargain, and by the time we do know, getting it back may not be possible. The deeper move is against the word progress itself. Kingsnorth does not want to call this progress. Progress, in his use, names a story we tell about technological change that conceals the trade-offs.

Directionality and the perennial: is this old or new?

The seminar pushed on whether the machine is a recent phenomenon or the oldest human one. Kingsnorth himself reaches back to Lewis Mumford, whose Myth of the Machine, written between 1967 and 1970, calls it the megamachine and traces its rise across centuries. Mumford observed in his own era that the human environment had been radically transformed in a short time, that the changes had altered the human personality, and that more radical transformations would follow if the process continued unchecked. The Mumford framing felt prophetic to participants, written before the cellphone, the smart speaker, the self-driving car, AI. Kingsnorth's project is partly to add himself to a lineage of figures who saw this coming.

The directionality question is harder. Is history moving somewhere? The room's instinct was wary of clean narratives. One participant cited a Dogon elder's framing that reality is neither a circle nor a spiral, just a circle: cyclical without progress. Another distinguished change from progress, granting that technology evolves but pushing back on whether the evolution is taking humanity anywhere worth going. Social media was named as a counterexample to community-building: more aggressive, more hateful, less the connector it was advertised to be.

A more biological framing came in. We have not changed substantially in 2,000 generations. What has changed is the imaginative bandwidth, what we deem possible, what knowledge we acquire. The directionality, on that view, is in the cognitive overlay rather than in the substrate. Underneath the layer of innovation, the perennial human problems persist.

This made the question sharper. Which problems are perennial, and which are genuinely new and require their own response. Cryptocurrency is new. AI is new. Whether they constitute progress is contested, but they are not nothing. The room landed on a parallax view: the tone of the warning is as old as time, but the topic keeps changing. Doomsday prophecies and versions of original sin have been around for thousands of years. What is in the foreground at any given moment shifts.

Depersonalized collectives and the loss of agency

The Kingsnorth passage that landed most heavily described the giant world-spanning corporations that now produce the food, clothes, technology, entertainment, and news of the present, while harvesting users as products through the personal data they freely provide. These corporations operate via global infrastructure, undersea cables, satellites, monitoring devices in homes and pockets, soon in streets and buildings. They are facilitated, on Kingsnorth's reading, by depersonalized states whose function is no longer to serve their citizens but to serve corporate interests under the heading of economic growth.

What Kingsnorth is feeling, the discussion noted, is helplessness. He still eats, wears clothes, communicates. The grievance is not that the goods are absent. It is that the agency to opt out is gone. We have moved from citizens to consumers. The dad who once ran his farm now goes to Home Depot because the farm cannot run without it.

Heidegger's question concerning technology surfaced as a parallel: every revealing is also a concealing, every new tool refocuses where humans pay attention and what they let go of. The trade-off framing is structurally Heideggerian. Several in the room flagged that Kingsnorth, writing from his Irish cottage with internet access, is in a similar position to the ones he criticizes. He is lamenting from inside the system. This is not a knockout objection; the lament is still legible and may still be true. But it is worth noticing.

The nostalgia trap

A persistent thread in the discussion pushed back on Kingsnorth's implicit timeline. His frame really only reaches back about six to eight thousand years, into the agricultural revolution and the rise of stockpiling. One participant argued that this is not spiritually ambitious enough. The deeper work, on this view, is older than written language, which was itself an innovation for tracking trade surpluses around 3,500 BC. The proposal was to look further back, to capacities of meaning-making that predate the brain's symbolic apparatus, to the kinds of bodily, sensual, and biomechanical knowledges that sustained relationality before symbolic technique began converting it into surplus energy.

The counter-pressure came back equally hard. There is a risk in this move that mirrors Kingsnorth's own. Both looking-backward gestures, the medieval one and the prehistoric one, can fall into a pattern where the friction of modernity drives a longing for a past harmony that may not be recoverable, or may not have existed in the form longed for. The challenge for anyone tempted by Kingsnorth's directionality is to be honest about how certain we are that the past utopia really was there, and that it can be brought back. The reading offered as a partial reply was that the harmony in the deep past, where it existed, was unconsensual; people paid attention to nature because the alternative was death. A consensual relation to nature, mediated through technology with explicit care for the relationality, would be novel rather than recovered.

The room noted, as a closing observation on Kingsnorth, that the perennial problems and the recent technological ones probably both need to be held at once. Some things are perennial. Some things are genuinely new. Confusing the categories produces bad responses in both directions.

Land's vocabulary: technomics, cybernetics, teleoplexy

The pivot to Land changed the temperature in the room. Land's essay is dense, neologism-heavy, and runs against the Kingsnorthian grain in a specific way. The session walked through the essay's vocabulary in roughly six steps.

The starting move is the intersection of technology and markets. Land's claim, drawing on Bohm-Bawerk's roundaboutness, is that under conditions of accumulating capital, technology and economics are not really separate domains. Excess material resources are diverted from immediate consumption into the enhancement of productive apparatus. Markets price things forward into the future. Technologies are spun off in response. The economic system updates. The loop continues. Land's name for the unified phenomenon is technomics. It is, in his vocabulary, capital's basic form.

The second move is cybernetics. A cybernetic system is a closed loop whose output feeds back into its input. The everyday example is a thermostat, whose signal up or down controls a mechanism that brings the room back to a setpoint. Land grants that technomics is cybernetic but insists on a critical distinction. A thermostat is homeostatically cybernetic; it pulls the system toward a stable state. Technomics is auto-catalytic; the more you put in, the more comes out. There is no setpoint. The system does not regulate itself, because, left to its own devices, markets see value in producing technology, technology then changes the human situation in ways that open further avenues for markets, and the spiral keeps going.

Land treats this as retro-causal. The framing should be held loosely, since Land may not mean it as literally as some readers take him, but the picture is that markets and technologies are time-traveling. They are pricing in things that do not yet exist, drawing them into the present, materializing as innovations whose appearance updates the price system, opening the next round.

The third move is the title concept: teleoplexy. Land treats technomics as having an emergent teleology, not just a feedback loop but a directionality, a purpose generated by the system rather than imposed on it. He calls this a deutero-teleology, or second-order teleology, repurposing purpose on purpose. The machine is not just running. It is going somewhere, and what we recognize in our daily lives as goals and ends are themselves channels that this larger thing is spinning off. Land says, in §08, that teleoplexy is indistinguishable from intelligence. This is what intelligence is, in Land's frame: an emergent system identifying and pursuing its own ends.

The prison and the governor problem

Land's most provocative single line in the essay, in §03, is that it is the prison, not the prisoner, who speaks. When we talk about what we are trying to do, we are not really speaking; the supervening system is speaking through us, directing our efforts by means that are at best faintly intelligible.

The room engaged this in several directions. One thread connected it to Gnostic motifs: a demiurgic system issuing its own directives, with people inside it unable to find a proximal cause. The thread was extended further to suggest that the technomic second-order phenomenon might itself be the construction of an interface for reconnection with what it has forgotten, the living spirit of the earth, embodied feminine wisdom. Whether Land would accept this framing is another question; he is explicitly post-human and treats humanity as a temporary substrate.

The major Landian move that set the room debating is on agency. Land wants to dispel the myth that we are governing the system. Humans think in homeostatic terms because the systems we look at do seem to correct: the earth does not randomly shoot off into space, things do not generally explode. This lulls us into the assumption that the economic loop is also subject to a governor, that we, or our institutions, are the thermostat. Kingsnorth wants the thermostat to work better. Land says there is no thermostat at all. The system is not the kind of system that has one.

A pushback came back: even if we are not currently in control, the recognition that we are not in control could itself be a source of agency. Land would answer, the room offered, that this is the prison speaking through the prisoner. The hypothesis is precisely that any apparent escape route is foreclosed in advance.

This is where Land turns out to be doing something specific. He is not advocating governance. He is also not advocating despair. He is proposing a science: the study of where the auto-catalytic loop is taking us, decoded from the price signals. Accelerationism, in his vocabulary, is this science. It is the project of reading the runaway system well enough to see its trajectory, not to control it.

Accelerationism as hypothesis

Land is careful, the session noted, to call accelerationism a hypothesis. To disconfirm it, you would have to identify what is doing the regulating and show how the system would ever stop. The fact that this is not easy to do is what Land takes as evidence, but he does not claim certainty. The framing is humble in its way. The bet is large, but it is being placed as a bet.

Several in the room pressed against accelerationism as a single-engine description. The pushback was that the picture assumes a monotheistic technomic machine when the actual situation is more polytheistic, with many auto-catalytic experiments running in different directions, competing, sometimes destroying each other, never resolving into a single trajectory. Land's response, charitably reconstructed, would be that competition still tends toward consolidation in the long run, that one auto-catalytic process tends to win out over its neighbors. The counter to that is that perfect knowledge never arrives, so the system never closes; it stays a complex emergent thing whose trajectory keeps being rewritten as new knowledge enters.

The room flagged what it found most disturbing about Land. He is explicitly post-human. He sees humanity as a temporary substrate, the way ants are a temporary substrate for the cordyceps fungus, drawing in something that will eventually replace us. The economy could continue trading with itself long after we are gone. Anyone signing up for the worldview, the room agreed, should be held to account for that consequence. It is not a popular position.

Land's framing of the operative question also landed. The humanist question is what kind of world do we want. The accelerationist question, in §13, is what can the earth do. The shift is from human preference to material possibility. The teleoplexy is asking what can be drawn from the earth to construct the next stage of the machine, and this question is independent of human flourishing. South Korea's birth rate can collapse to zero and the question still gets asked.

Money, the labyrinth, and the closing tensions

Money came up late but pointedly. Land treats money in §11 as both his greatest conceptual resource and his most ineluctable problem. Price signals do glimpse the future, in the sense that valuations move ahead of the actual, integrating forecasts. There is signal in there. But there is also a great deal of noise. Humans are fickle, demand changes, money is printed, prices update on the basis of circumstances unrelated to the underlying reality, and the signal is scrambled. Land calls money a labyrinth: it locally untangles to enable transactions while globally entangling, with point-of-use illusions that money represents the world.

A question from the room asked whether the issue is fiat currency specifically, given how the choice to denominate value in fiat detaches the economic system from material reality. Land's answer, as best as could be reconstructed, is that the problem is monetary in general. Fiat or otherwise, the fact that money satisfies needs and desires through exchange is itself a purpose that pollutes Land's preferred purpose for markets, which is reading the teleoplectic direction. Cryptocurrency, on this account, would not be a solution either; it just relocates the labyrinth.

The session ran into closing tensions. One participant raised the simplest possible counter to Land: if you do not like the runaway machine, turn off the power. Cut the data centers. Shut down the cloud. The Kaczynski option, in shorthand. The reply was honest; the participant who voiced approval also volunteered that they did not want to go to jail. The line landed because it pointed at something genuine. The hypothesis that we cannot escape coexists with the practical fact that physical infrastructure can in principle be turned off. Whether anyone will, and at what cost, is a different question.

Another participant arrived at the so-what. If we are prisoners in a prison we cannot escape, why bother developing the science of where the prison is going. The Land response, as the session reconstructed it, is that the science is contemplative. The point is to understand. Whether you participate in the project is up to you, but it is going where it is going regardless.

The session closed without resolution, which was the right shape. Kingsnorth and Land are not commensurable. They are not asking the same question. Kingsnorth wants the trade-offs to be visible so that we can choose differently. Land does not believe choosing differently is on the table; he wants the trajectory to be readable. What they share is the conviction that something large is happening that exceeds the agency of any single human or institution to redirect. The seminar's job was to hold both stances long enough to see where they each cut, and where they each fail. The room did that and stopped on time.
