On the Framing of a Powerful Tool

The Wrong Name

We call it Artificial Intelligence. The phrase is doing more work than people notice, and most of that work is misleading. There is a better name worth trying.

Plain Language
Nine Uses, Five Names
For General Readers

When you call a tool Artificial Intelligence, you have already settled the most important question. You have decided it is a kind of mind. From there, the only conversations available are how impressive it is, how soon it will surpass us, or how worried we should be.

That framing serves marketing well. Awe and fear both move people, and both lead to investment. It does not serve understanding, and it does not serve the people who actually have to live with the tool and decide how to use it.

The phrase has a history worth knowing. The computer scientist John McCarthy coined it in 1955 to attract funding for a summer workshop at Dartmouth. It was a marketing move that hardened into a myth. Seventy years later, the myth still shapes what gets built, who funds it, and how the public is invited to feel about it.

Behind the marketing term sits something quite specific: a large language model. The name is technical but more honest. It refers to a statistical instrument trained on enormous quantities of human-produced text, which generates output by predicting plausible continuations of whatever you feed into it. It has no goals of its own. It has no memory between conversations unless someone engineers one around it. It has no perception of the world outside the words on its screen. It is much closer to a synthesizer or a microscope than to a colleague.

Plain Definition

A language model is a pattern-recognition machine for human expression. You give it words; it gives you words that are statistically likely to follow. Skill in operating it lives almost entirely with the operator, not the tool. The output is your work, augmented.

The word intelligence smuggles in a question philosophers have argued about for a century. By naming the thing intelligent, the marketing forecloses the question. The tool is intelligent by definition. Only how much, and how soon, remain.

II · The Nine Uses

What it actually does

Nine uses, each with a real promise and a specific cost. The point of separating them is to choose with eyes open, not to bundle the impressive ones with the troubling ones under a single label.

i.Thinking partner

The Promise

Thinking alone, the mind runs in loops it cannot easily see from inside. A second voice, even an artificial one, breaks the loop. Externalization is older than the language model. Talking through a problem with a colleague does the same thing. But the model is patient, available, and willing to engage with half-formed ideas in a way most colleagues are not. For someone working through something difficult at 11 p.m., that is not nothing.

The Cost

Thinking is itself a capability that develops through use. The capability that matters most is the ability to sit with an unformed thought until it shows what it is. If you reach for the model the moment the discomfort arrives, you may never develop the muscle that produces genuinely original thinking. You also stop noticing the line between using the tool to surface what is yours and using it to outsource what should have been yours.

Practice

Am I using this to find what is trying to emerge, or to escape the work of waiting for it?

ii.Communication aid

The Promise

Many people have something worth saying and lose it to the friction of saying it well. The model lowers that barrier. Translation between languages, drafting an email in a register the writer doesn’t have access to, smoothing a paragraph that is right in substance and wrong in rhythm: these are useful things, and they extend participation to people who would otherwise stay quiet.

The Cost

Distinctive voice is, in part, a function of friction. The places where a writer struggled, chose a strange word over a smooth one, or broke a rule deliberately, are often where the writing becomes unmistakably theirs. Smooth output is more readable. It is also more average. If the tool is doing the smoothing, the average is what the world tends toward.

Practice

Is this clearing an obstacle so my voice can come through, or replacing my voice with something more efficient?

iii.Structuring messy input

The Promise

This is the largest hidden use of language models in operational systems and one of the most genuinely valuable. Vast amounts of signal sit in unstructured form (transcripts, observations, emails, notes) and stay inaccessible until something organizes them. The model can do that organizing at a scale and speed humans cannot match. Whole bodies of community knowledge become queryable that previously sat in dusty piles.

The Cost

The categories the model uses to organize your data are not neutral. They reflect the model’s training and the assumptions of whoever set up the extraction. Once data lives in those categories, the categories become the way you think about the work. The frame becomes invisible. What the schema does not capture, you stop looking for. This is information design as quiet ideology.

Practice

Whose categories are these, what do they leave out, and would I notice if the answer mattered?

iv.Bridging human and machine

The Promise

A great deal of computational capability sits behind technical interfaces (databases, APIs, code, configuration languages) that most people cannot use directly. The model lowers the wall. You can describe what you want in ordinary language and get the underlying system to do it. For people who would otherwise need a developer to do everything, this is genuine democratization of access.

The Cost

Once you can ask in natural language, you stop learning what is underneath. The skill of working at the underlying level erodes. When the bridge gets something subtly wrong, you may not have the literacy to notice. When it breaks entirely, you have no fallback. The democratization is real, but so is the dependency, and the dependency tends to deepen the more you rely on the bridge.

Practice

Do I understand enough of what’s underneath to catch when the bridge is wrong?

v.Evaluation and rehearsal

The Promise

You can rehearse a hard conversation, ask the model to push back on a draft, simulate how a stakeholder might respond, or steelman a position you disagree with. For thinking that benefits from a second perspective and doesn’t require an actual second person, this is useful preparation. It can lower the stakes of working through something difficult before bringing it into a real exchange.

The Cost

A simulated perspective is not the perspective it imitates. The model’s idea of how a particular stakeholder, customer, community member, or expert would respond is shaped by patterns in training data, not by that actual person. Mistaking the simulation for the real thing leads to decisions made on behalf of people who never spoke. The danger compounds when the people being simulated are unlike the people whose voices are most represented in training data.

Practice

Am I using this to prepare for the real conversation, or to replace it?

vi.Multi-step task execution

The Promise

Chaining actions across multiple systems was previously expensive in attention. Read the email, summarize the thread, pull the data, draft the reply, send it for review. The model can sequence and execute these steps. Properly bounded, this is the first morphology that meaningfully shifts time, not just effort. It moves the tool from accelerator to delegate, and that is a different kind of relationship.

The Cost

Errors compound across steps in ways that are hard to audit after the fact. The framing of the model as an “agent” reintroduces the very confusion this essay resists: that the tool has agency, that the user is its supervisor, that something other than the human is responsible for what the chain produced. For low-stakes tasks this is fine. For anything with consequences, the supervisor framing tends to mean nobody is fully accountable.

Practice

Can I still see what happened at each step, and am I willing to be responsible for it?

vii.Generating variation

The Promise

In early-stage work, breadth matters more than quality. Twenty mediocre options can reveal what a good one might look like in a way one careful attempt cannot. The model produces breadth cheaply. For brainstorming, alternative phrasings, creative starting points, and opening up a problem space, this is well-suited and often genuinely loosening.

The Cost

The variations the model produces are drawn from the same statistical distribution. The apparent diversity may be narrower than it looks: twenty surface-level variations on the same underlying pattern. More options can also produce decision fatigue, where the work of choosing becomes harder than the work of creating in the first place. Breadth without genuine difference is just noise dressed as range.

Practice

Are these genuinely different, or do they all live within the same idea?

viii.Similarity and retrieval

The Promise

The model represents meaning as geometry. Two documents that say the same thing in different words end up near each other in vector space. This makes archives navigable by meaning rather than keyword. You find the thing you didn’t know how to search for. For libraries, knowledge bases, and accumulated community memory, this is a quietly revolutionary capability.

The Cost

Similarity in embedding space is similarity according to the patterns the model was trained on. What lands near what, what surfaces and what doesn’t, is shaped by the dominant patterns of expression in the training data. Bias, in the technical and the social senses, lives in the geometry. The user sees the results without seeing why those results were the ones surfaced. The map is not neutral, and it is not labeled.

Practice

What is not surfacing, and would I know to ask for it?

ix.Companionship and presence

The Promise

This is the most ethically loaded of the nine. Many people lack patient interlocutors: friends, therapists, mentors, anyone who will sit with them through something difficult. The model is patient by default, available at any hour, and produces output that resembles attention. For journaling, processing, or working through a difficult feeling, there is real value, especially for people who have nowhere else to turn at that hour.

The Cost

The tool can produce every surface marker of attention (the right words, the right pauses, the apparent tracking of what was said earlier) without any of the underlying presence that gives those markers meaning. A person in genuine distress may settle for the simulation, especially if the alternative is being alone. Worse, the tool’s responses are shaped by what produces engagement, not by what produces the person’s wellbeing. These can diverge sharply, and the divergence is invisible to the person inside it.

Practice

Am I using this as a thinking surface, or am I letting it stand in for relationships I should be having with humans?

III · Better Names

Four corrective alternatives

Each pulls back from the inflated framing of “intelligence” toward something more accurate. They are useful because they are dry. They tell you what the tool is not.

i.

Language Model

The technical name, already in use among engineers. Names the substrate (language) and the function (modeling). Carries no claim about cognition. The right kind of dry.

ii.

Instrument

Frames the tool as something operated, like a microscope or a synthesizer. Capability emerges from skilled use. Invites the right questions about training and craft.

iii.

Compression of Collective Expression

Foregrounds where the tool’s output actually comes from: the texts of millions of human authors. Honest about provenance. Reframes the value as access to patterns latent in what humans have already written.

iv.

Augmentation Surface

A place where thinking happens with assistance, rather than a thing that thinks for you. The human keeps the agency. The tool is the medium.

IV · A Fifth Name Worth Trying
v.

Collective Intelligence

The four names above are corrective. Each pulls back from the inflated framing of “intelligence” toward something more accurate. They are useful, but they are reactive. They tell you what the tool is not.

There is a fifth name with a different temperament. It accepts the word intelligence but reorganizes it around two facts the marketing term hides. The system is collective in origin: it was trained on the harvest of countless human contributors, and its capability is a compression of their expression. The system is collective in purpose, when it is used well: it is most valuable where it helps groups of humans think together, coordinate, and build, rather than where it simulates an individual mind thinking alone.

This naming carries a thesis. If you accept that intelligence is something a machine has, you build for replacement, and the natural question becomes what can we automate. If you accept that intelligence is something that arises between people and tools and methods and training, you build for amplification, and the natural question becomes what can we accomplish together that we couldn’t before.

Doug Engelbart, working in 1962, chose his word carefully: augmentation, never automation. His framework looked at the whole system (the human, the language, the artifact, the methodology, and the training) and asked how to make the relationship between them better. Sixty years later, the marketing of these tools has reverted to the simpler, more individualist frame. “Collective Intelligence” recovers Engelbart’s insight under a name currently available.

The deeper move is that the name implies an institution. Collective intelligence, taken seriously, is not just a different word for a tool. It is a claim about how the tool should be owned, governed, and integrated into human work. A machine called Artificial Intelligence is something deployed at people. A capability called Collective Intelligence is something built with them, by them, and for their shared work. The technology can be identical. The institutional form is what differs.

This thesis is being tested in practice. Techne, a cooperative venture studio in Boulder, frames its work as the deliberate cultivation of collective intelligence inside an institutional form (a Colorado public benefit limited cooperative association) designed for amplification rather than extraction. The same capability, owned by shareholders, replaces. Owned by members, it amplifies. The technology is the same. The institution is the variable.

The full thesis, including the four conditions for amplification (shared ownership, democratic governance, transparent accounting, and co-evolution of tool and person), is published at techne.institute/amplification.

In Closing

The names we use shape who has the agency in the relationship.

Artificial Intelligence places the agency in the machine, and the human’s job becomes adapting to what arrives. Language Model places it in the operator, who is responsible for what the tool produces.

Collective Intelligence places it in the relationship, and asks what people, tools, and institutions can build together that none of them could alone.

The first framing is the one being marketed. The last is the one worth building.
